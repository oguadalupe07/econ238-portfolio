## The Proposition

### Genesee River Agricultural Conservation Partnership Initiative

**Official Ballot Question**

Shall the City of Rochester establish a conservation program beginning January 2028, funded by a $0.50 monthly household fee for three years, to compensate willing upstream farmers for converting selected riverside farmland into protective vegetation? Projects would be selected based on expected pollution reductions and cost-effectiveness. Farmers would maintain these areas for at least ten years, with sufficient funding reserved before contracts are signed. The household fee would expire December 2030 unless separately renewed.

---

## What's the Deal?

The Genesee River runs through Rochester, but some of the pollution affecting it originates farther upstream. Agricultural runoff can carry soil, fertilizers, and other nutrients into the river, affecting water quality and surrounding ecosystems. One potential solution is to replace portions of riverside farmland with trees, shrubs, and grasses that help filter runoff before it reaches the water. However, farmers sacrifice productive land and potential income by doing so.

This initiative proposes a tradeoff: Rochester households would contribute $0.50 per month for three years to compensate willing upstream farmers for maintaining these protective areas for at least ten years. The program would prioritize projects expected to reduce the most pollution relative to their cost. In exchange for a small household expense, Rochester residents could benefit from a healthier river, while farmers would receive compensation for the land they give up.

## Best Case for YES

In the best-case scenario, Rochester residents would help reduce pollution in the Genesee River by paying upstream farmers to protect environmentally sensitive farmland. Instead of allowing soil and fertilizer runoff to enter nearby waterways, vegetated buffers would help capture pollutants before they reach the river. Farmers would receive compensation for giving up productive land, making conservation financially worthwhile rather than an additional burden. By prioritizing projects with the greatest expected pollution reduction per dollar spent, Rochester could achieve meaningful environmental improvements without purchasing farmland or requiring farmers to participate. The three-year funding limit would also prevent an indefinite household expense, while ten-year conservation agreements would provide benefits beyond the payment period. If successful, the program could demonstrate how voluntary cooperation between upstream farmers and downstream residents can address pollution that neither group has sufficient incentive to solve independently.

## Best Case for NO

In the best-case scenario for voting NO, Rochester avoids committing approximately $1.68 million to a program whose environmental benefits are difficult to guarantee. Although vegetated buffers can reduce agricultural runoff, their effectiveness depends on location, soil conditions, and farmer participation. The city could spend substantial amounts without producing noticeable improvements in the Genesee River, especially if other pollution sources remain unaddressed. Rochester households would also avoid paying for improvements on privately owned farmland, particularly when existing state and federal conservation programs already offer financial assistance. Rather than creating another publicly funded program, the city could direct its limited resources toward more immediate local priorities or encourage farmers to use existing conservation incentives. Voting NO would preserve household income and municipal flexibility while avoiding the financial and administrative risks of an additional conservation program.

## Where Is the Body Buried?

The biggest uncertainty is whether Rochester would receive enough environmental benefits to justify approximately $1.68 million in spending. Our preliminary target of 150 acres is not based on a complete watershed assessment, and the estimated $150 annual payment per acre may not convince farmers to participate. Buffer effectiveness also varies by location, meaning the city could spend substantial amounts without achieving noticeable improvements downstream. Administrative expenses, maintenance, and monitoring could exceed projections. The proposal also assumes Rochester could legally establish the household fee and compensate farmers outside city limits, neither of which has been confirmed.

## Why Isn't This Already Happening?

The biggest obstacle is that the costs and benefits of conservation fall on different people. Farmers would lose productive land and income by establishing protective vegetation, while much of the environmental benefit would extend downstream to people who are not paying for it. Rochester residents also have little incentive to contribute voluntarily when they could enjoy a cleaner river without paying, creating a free-rider problem. Organizing agreements between thousands of households and individual farmers would involve substantial transaction costs, including negotiating payments, identifying suitable land, and monitoring compliance. Although state and federal conservation programs already exist, they have not eliminated agricultural runoff throughout the watershed. Uncertain environmental benefits and competing public spending priorities also make additional funding difficult to justify. This initiative attempts to overcome those barriers by pooling household contributions and coordinating voluntary conservation agreements.

## What Would the Program Cost?

| Item | Proposed amount |
|---|---|
| Monthly household fee | $0.50 |
| Annual household cost | $6 |
| Total household cost over three years | $18 |
| Fee collection period | January 2028–December 2030 |
| Estimated gross program revenue | $1.68 million |
| Preliminary conservation target | Approximately 150 acres |
| Minimum conservation agreement | 10 years |

The gross revenue estimate assumes approximately 93,261 paying Rochester households for 36 months:

**93,261 × $0.50 × 36 = $1,678,698**

The program would prioritize conservation projects based on expected pollution reductions relative to cost, rather than guaranteeing a fixed number of acres.

A historical USDA New York payment schedule provides a planting benchmark of approximately $3,801 per acre for one forest-buffer practice. Applying that rate to 150 acres gives an illustrative establishment estimate of $570,150.

If farmers received an illustrative $150 per acre annually, ten years of compensation across 150 acres would total $225,000.

These estimates exclude uncertainty about actual site conditions, participation, and implementation costs. The $150 payment rate and 150-acre goal are planning assumptions, not verified project bids. The city would need to reserve funds for the full cost of each agreement before signing it.

When Did We Decide This Was Normal?
By Olivia Guadalupe




The fictional Meliora data center in my environmental economics assignment uses hundreds of millions of gallons of water every year, hundreds of millions of kilowatt-hours of electricity, millions of dekatherms of natural gas, and produces thousands of tons of waste. To that information,  my immediate reaction is, Jesus Christ, humanity is the worst! Initially, I didn't calculate anything. Rather I saw a resource followed by a ridiculous number of zeros and pretty much decided nobody should be allowed to use that much of anything. Then I started looking at the University of Rochester, which was annoying because apparently I have never held the place I actually live to the same standard. UR reports its water use, energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, waste, transportation, and other environmental measures through STARS. I had never looked at it or even knew what it was until yesterday. I've never walked past Strong Memorial Hospital wondering how much water it used that morning. If somebody told me Strong used a gigantic amount, my instinct would probably be well yeah, it's a hospital. I would give it the benefit of the doubt before even knowing the number. I gave Meliora basically none, because boo data centers!
At first I thought this was necessary versus unnecessary resource use. Hospitals need water. Farms need water. Data centers use water so somebody can ask ChatGPT what  to make their newest AI flyer that always looks atrocious. George ruined that by asking me about corn. We have to eat, sure, but corn also becomes animal feed, ethanol, sweeteners, and plenty of things nobody technically needs. Data centers also support hospitals, universities, banks, businesses, cloud storage, and most of the internet I use constantly. It gets even more embarrassing if I keep going. I drive a Tesla, whose giant battery makes it heavier and can contribute to faster tire wear.  I buy things I don't need. I attend a university with a giant physical campus where I really go to 4 places regularly, dorm-> library->Dining hall->class->Repeat! I care deeply about nature, hell I cried and screamed at my friend for killing a spider that was outside because HE was scared about it! I’m sure I look nuts to most, but apparently not enough to stop wanting a pretty resource-intensive human life.
There is a river near where I grew up in New Jersey that makes this much harder for me. I love the Third River (Near Nutley/Clifton NJ). I've walked through it, taken pictures around it, looked for old bottles in it, picked garbage out of it, and spent an amount of time in that water that probably makes the next fact slightly concerning. The U.S. Geological Survey is studying the Second and Third Rivers because high levels of microbial contamination, including E. coli, have been found in them. The Third River is also a tributary of the Passaic River. Farther downstream, the Lower Passaic has its own much uglier history of industrial contamination involving dioxin, mercury, PCBs, and other pollutants. It's a Superfund site, although, coming from New Jersey, that distinction is unfortunately not very exclusive. We have 115 of them! The more the merrier! Even Senator  Cory Booker said, “For far too long, the Passaic River has remained one of the most contaminated waterways in our nation,”. Basically, I love a river that I would not recommend swimming in, even though I have gotten in it plenty of times. That pisses me off. I don't remember watching some pristine Third River become contaminated. This is just the river I know. Whatever happened to get it here happened before I had any say in what I thought a clean river should look like. “Don't get the water in your mouth” doesn't feel like something was taken from me because I never expected anything different. I can still take pictures, find bottles, watch animals, and love being there. I have adjusted perfectly well to having a river I probably shouldn't swim in, which is a pretty horrible definition of adapting to your environment.
My emotional answer is obvious: clean it. I want my river to be clean. Unfortunately, economics makes even that answer annoying. How clean? At what cost? Where is the contamination coming from? What infrastructure would have to change, and who should pay for it? If New Jersey had another $10 million for environmental improvements, would I really insist that my river deserves it more than reducing lead exposure, improving air quality, or restoring several other waterways? Probably not. If that money could do much more somewhere else, “but I love this one” isn't a very good argument. Which means there is apparently some amount of pollution in my river that I am willing to accept. I hate that conclusion. I don't know what that amount is. I know spending infinite money until the Third River is pristine isn't reasonable, but if somebody told me the economically efficient outcome was a river where kids still shouldn't swim, I would be furious. Somewhere between those positions is a level of damage I am apparently willing to tolerate because preventing it costs too much. Saying I care about clean water was considerably nicer before I had to put a margin on it. Even that assumes we're counting the costs correctly. If preventing pollution is “too expensive,” expensive to whom? If somebody saves money while people downstream get worse water, the cheap option isn't actually cheap. Someone is still paying; they just might be paying with something that never appears on a bill. On the other hand, maybe loving one particular river makes me want everyone else to spend more saving it than the benefits can justify. I don't particularly like either possibility.
That brought me back to Meliora. Suppose Rochester somehow charged the data center for the full social cost of its water, electricity, pollution, and infrastructure, and Meliora paid the bill and continued using an absolutely horrifying quantity of resources. Would I be okay with it then? I genuinely don't know. If my problem is externalities, I should be much happier. But part of me still wants to say nobody should use that much in the first place. Maybe I don't actually believe every environmental resource should go to whoever is willing to pay its full cost. Or maybe a gigantic number is still screwing with my judgment. What bothers me most is how easily any of this can eventually stop feeling unusual. I inherited a Third River where swimming already seemed like a bad idea. I also inherited hospitals, highways, farms, universities, and power plants whose enormous resource demands mostly feel normal. If Meliora gets built, maybe thirty years from now people depend on its jobs, taxes, infrastructure, and services too. Someone could point out its ridiculous electricity consumption and people might respond the same way I responded to Strong: well obviously, what do you expect? I still think Meliora's gigantic numbers should make me uncomfortable. I just don't think being uncomfortable is enough anymore. I want to know what we're giving up, who benefits, who pays, what the alternatives are, and what people do when those costs actually reach them. But I also want to keep asking why some environmental costs horrify me while others have been around long enough that I barely notice them. I don't have a satisfying answer for how much damage humans should accept. I am part of the demand creating it, and even for a river I love, apparently my answer isn't zero. What scares me more is how easy it is to stop noticing that we ever made that choice.

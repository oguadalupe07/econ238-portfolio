
So Why Do We Keep Moving Toward the Water?
By Olivia Guadalupe
When I started this, I was pretty convinced I knew where I was going. Extreme weather is dangerous, people keep moving toward the water anyway, and apparently nobody has learned anything. Every time I see houses sitting approximately three feet from the ocean after a hurricane, part of me thinks, WHY ARE WE STILL BUILDING THERE? We have an enormous country. Go inland! Go to Vermont! Problem solved. Unfortunately, then I looked at the deaths. I combined National Weather Service data on tornado, flood, and hurricane fatalities going back to 1940. I expected some horrifying upward trend. Instead, the 1940s averaged about 262 deaths per year, compared with about 195 in the 2010s. Then I remembered there were way fewer Americans in 1940, which made my argument even worse. Adjusted for population, fatalities fell from about 1.88 deaths per million people in the 1940s to 0.61 in the 2010s.
Apparently my graph about humans putting themselves in danger had become a graph about how good humans are at not dying from it. That doesn't mean the hazard disappeared. We changed the consequences of being exposed to it. Forecasting, warnings, buildings, evacuations, and emergency response improved. Instead of dealing with risk entirely by avoiding dangerous places, we got better at living in them. Which creates an annoying possibility: getting better at protecting ourselves from environmental risk can make us more willing to live with environmental risk. And apparently we are VERY willing. The population of U.S. coastline counties increased from about 47.4 million in 1960 to 87.4 million in 2008. That doesn't mean 40 million people ran directly into floodplains, but it definitely ruined my “everybody go inland” solution. Cape May ruined it even more.
I love Cape May. If somebody offered me two identical houses and one was near the ocean while the other was in the safest random inland town imaginable, I genuinely don't know that I'm picking the inland one. So apparently I am one of the idiots from my introduction. Except, am I? I was treating minimizing environmental risk as if it were everybody's objective. It isn't. Cape May gives people beaches, recreation, jobs, tourism, community, and simply the enjoyment of living near the ocean. Those benefits have value too. Minimizing risk and maximizing welfare are not the same thing. People can rationally accept some additional risk because they value what they get in return. Then economics has to make that answer annoying too: who actually pays for the risk? If I get all the benefits of my Cape May house but some flood costs eventually fall on disaster assistance, insurance arrangements, infrastructure spending, or other taxpayers, my private cost can be lower than the social cost. Suddenly, “the beach is worth the risk to me” doesn't necessarily mean the beach is worth the full cost of my decision. Great.
And then Burke et al. made my nice fatality graphs annoying too. They estimate roughly 120,000 U.S. deaths annually from suboptimal temperatures, which makes a few hundred annual storm fatalities look microscopic. Except I can't put 120,000 vs. 200!!! in giant letters and call it a day. Burke statistically estimates temperature-attributable mortality; National Weather Service fatalities are attributed to specific events. They aren't directly comparable. So my graphs don't prove that we conquered weather. They show something narrower: we have gotten much better at surviving certain hazards while continuing to expose ourselves to them. I started this thinking the environmentally responsible answer was to stop living in risky places. I don't think that anymore. Moving everybody inland has costs too: lost jobs, communities, recreation, tourism, amenities, and places people genuinely love. But “people like the coast, let them live there” isn't enough either. I want to know who gets the benefits, who actually pays when something goes wrong, and whether adaptation reduces risk enough that we respond by exposing even more people and property to it. So no, I don't think everyone needs to move to Vermont. I would still live in Cape May. Apparently now I just want to know whether I am actually paying for my Cape May decision, or whether I get the ocean view while somebody in Vermont gets part of the bill.
Sorry, Vermont.

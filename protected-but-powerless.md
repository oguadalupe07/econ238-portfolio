# Protected, But Powerless?

### Who Gets Protected, Who Benefits, and Who Gets to Decide?

*ECON 238 – Environmental Economics – Fall 2026*

Environmental policies are usually designed to make people's lives better. But what happens when the people benefiting from those policies aren't necessarily the ones making the decisions?

From a $25,000 housing rehabilitation program in Mount Holly, New Jersey, to Indigenous land rights and university land acknowledgments, my research explores how property rights influence who receives assistance, who benefits from environmental improvements, and who has control over the land they live on.

I also wanted to see how other people would approach these decisions. It's easy to support environmental justice in theory, but what happens when you're the person who has to make the trade-offs?

## Part 2: You Make the Rules!

### An Interactive Environmental Justice Experiment

Imagine you're in charge of making decisions about housing, environmental protection, and land rights.

You have limited funding, competing interests, and people who will be affected by whatever you decide.

**What would you do?**

I created a five-question interactive experiment where participants make decisions about:

1. Who deserves $25,000 in housing assistance?
2. What happens when environmental improvements make housing more expensive?
3. Should Indigenous communities have greater control over federally protected land?
4. What should universities do beyond land acknowledgments?
5. Should Indigenous nations have greater authority over national parks?

There aren't necessarily right or wrong answers. Each choice involves economic trade-offs, and even well-intentioned decisions can create unintended consequences.

## Part 3: So, Who Actually Wins?

One thing I noticed while working on this assignment is that environmental justice isn't always about choosing between a good policy and a bad one.

Sometimes, two groups have legitimate interests that conflict. Sometimes, protecting one group creates costs for another. And sometimes, the people making the decisions aren't the ones who have to live with the consequences.

That's why I wanted to make the Google Form. Rather than just reading about the trade-offs, participants have to make some of those decisions themselves.

**The question I want people to leave with is this:**

*Can a policy truly protect a community if that community has little say in how it works?*
